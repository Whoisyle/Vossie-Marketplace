1. Executive summary
What the evidence currently supports, what remains a hypothesis, and
where EduHackers can differentiate.
The Hack Jam brief asks teams to design a digital Marketplace for Incubation Hub entrepreneurs and
the wider student community, covering profiles, listings, discovery, interaction, orders or enquiries,
safety, accessibility and value creation. It also judges solutions on innovation, genuine user need,
practicality, scalability, accessibility and sustainability. Those criteria mean a generic listing board is
not enough. [R1]
Desk research shows that many individual features the team initially considered are already present
in the market. UWC has built a student marketplace with verification, search optimisation and
security testing. Makethe supports verified student stores, products, services, bookings, secure
payments, analytics and CRM. Campus Needs combines campus verification with protected payments
and on-campus handoff. Grid has a wanted board. KANU combines entrepreneurship learning with a
school marketplace and real venture outcomes. CampuzBhiz combines marketplace, food delivery,
rider tracking and events. Therefore EduHackers should not claim novelty for verification, listings,
messaging, requests, bookings, delivery, events or basic analytics in isolation. [R4] [R5] [R6] [R7]
[R11] [R12]
The strongest white-space hypothesis is the closed loop between demand, venture creation and
incubation. Instead of treating a failed search or stock-out as the end of the user journey, the
proposed system tries local alternatives, other sellers, other campuses, substitute products or service
slots, and finally a structured request. Repeated unmet demand becomes an opportunity signal. That
signal can help an existing seller extend their offer, help an aspiring entrepreneur choose what to
test, and help the Incubation Hub identify where supply or business capability is weak. Real
transaction outcomes then feed back into support decisions.
Strategic thesis
EduHackers should position the concept as a verified Eduvos entrepreneurship operating
system: campus-first commerce + protected transactions + demand intelligence +
evidence-driven incubation. The novelty claim should be modest: this exact closed loop was not
found in the reviewed public student-marketplace implementations; that does not prove global
uniqueness.
The proposed concept is intentionally broad for research. Any verified Eduvos student can be a buyer
and, after ID + student verification, a seller. The research scope includes products, services, food,
second-hand goods, rentals, digital products, selected subscriptions, events, gigs, group orders, a
campus-first short-video discovery feed, student couriers, designated pickup points, protected
payments, buyer and seller trust, AI-assisted compliance, and an Incubation Hub intelligence layer.
The MVP should not attempt all of these at full production depth; the research must determine which
elements are essential to prove the ecosystem.
A major finding is that the proposed 2% platform commission is not the same as an all-in payment
cost. Current public pricing is roughly 2.9% + R1 for Paystack local cards, 3.2% + R2 for Payfast
cards, 2% for some EFT rails, and 1.5% minimum R1 for several Ozow pay-by-bank / PayShap
methods. If Eduvos absorbs processor fees, a 2% commission can be negative-margin on card
payments. The commercial model must therefore decide whether processing is passed through,
subsidised, negotiated at enterprise rates, or incorporated into a different fee architecture. [R19]
[R21] [R22]
The recommendation is to use Week 2 research to validate the biggest uncertainties rather than
perfect every feature. Priority validation questions are: how Eduvos students currently discover
student businesses; what makes them trust a seller; whether meaningful unmet demand exists

3. Method, evidence status and research
discipline
This report combines four evidence types: the uploaded Hack Jam brief; public Eduvos information;
current market/prior-art research; and external academic, regulatory and industry sources. A fifth
layer - EduHackers product-policy modelling - captures decisions made during the team discovery
discussion. These layers must not be confused.
Evidence labels used throughout
• Brief requirement: directly supported by the Eduvos Hack Jam brief.
• External evidence: supported by public academic, industry, provider or regulatory material.
• Prior art: an existing product demonstrates that a feature is already in market.
• Research hypothesis: plausible direction that still needs Eduvos user/staff evidence.
• Policy proposal: a rule the team has discussed, but which requires legal, operational or user validation
before production.
The most important discipline is falsification. Interviews should not be used to make respondents
agree with a pre-built solution. Instead, the team should look for evidence that its assumptions are
wrong. If students already have effective discovery channels, or Incubation Hub staff cannot use
marketplace data, or cross-campus fulfilment is too expensive, the product should adapt. This is
stronger research than selectively collecting supportive quotes.
Research limitation
Desk research cannot establish how Eduvos students actually behave. Claims such as
“students rely on WhatsApp” remain hypotheses until observed across actual Eduvos users.
Findings should be segmented by campus and user type, and any Potchefstroom-heavy sample
must be labelled as such.
Source quality and freshness
For current market/pricing claims, this dossier prioritises official provider pages and 2026 sources.
Payment fees, app features and regulatory guidance can change; they should be re-checked before
implementation. Academic studies provide context rather than direct proof of Eduvos behaviour.

6. Eduvos context: one institution, many
campus micro-markets
Eduvos publicly operates 12 campuses. The 2025 annual report presents a national student footprint
with major differences in campus size, plus an online cohort. These differences matter because a
marketplace can be liquid at a large campus while remaining sparse at a smaller campus. A single

The same annual report indicates an Incubation Hub pilot at Midrand, Tygervalley and Nelson
Mandela Bay involving 22 students, which is directly relevant to the Hack Jam: the marketplace can

marketplace evidence rather than only periodic self-reporting. [R2]
Eduvos also demonstrates an ability to run institution-wide engagement. In 2026, its career fairs took
place across all 12 campuses, with more than 1,700 students and 240 employers participating. That

operations would be identical everywhere. [R3]

7. Studentpreneurship evidence in South
Africa
South African studentpreneurship research gives useful context but should not be treated as direct
Eduvos evidence. Fihla and Gavaza’s 2026 qualitative work with 15 studentpreneurs found that
entrepreneurship support mechanisms could be uneven, poorly integrated and inconsistently
accessible, and argued for more sustained support. [R36]
Research across South African public universities has highlighted mentorship, training, networking,
business advice, creative spaces, management support and funding as important support
mechanisms. Another study of transitions from student to business owner identified
resourcing/capability challenges and reliance on informal support networks. [R37] [R38]
Implication for the marketplace research
These findings justify testing whether a marketplace can become more than a transaction layer. If a
platform can observe real demand, fulfilment, repeat customers, cancellations, seller response time
and opportunity gaps, it may provide a practical evidence layer for entrepreneurship support. The key
word is may: Incubation Hub interviews must determine whether those metrics are meaningful,
ethical and actionable in Eduvos practice.
Questions to validate
• Do current student entrepreneurs have reliable customer-acquisition channels?
• Which support needs appear only after a student starts trading?
• Would evidence of real traction improve mentorship decisions?
• Would aspiring entrepreneurs use campus demand evidence to choose what to test?
• Where would marketplace analytics create unfair or misleading judgments?

9. South African payment behaviour and
checkout design
Card remains important, but pay-by-bank, instant EFT, PayShap and digital wallets are increasingly
relevant. Peach Payments’ 2026 reporting says digital wallets grew more than 35% year-on-year,
40.8% of surveyed retailers were already on Instant EFT or PayShap, and Capitec Pay represented
around 40% of payment value at some processors. [R18]
The research implication is not “add every payment logo.” It is to make checkout recognise familiar
student payment methods, recover cleanly from network failures, show fees before commitment, and
use a payment orchestration architecture that can change providers without rewriting the
marketplace. Crypto can remain a research/future rail, but it introduces reporting, volatility and
compliance complexity that should not distract from the MVP.
Payment methods to compare in user research
• Debit / credit cards and mobile wallets where supported.
• Pay-by-bank / instant EFT / PayShap-style rails.
• Standard EFT only where reconciliation can be made reliable.
• Crypto as a future/high-complexity option, not a default MVP dependency.
Conversion risk
Payment choice should be evidence-driven. The 2026 online-retail report identifies card
declines and shipping fees among major abandonment causes. The marketplace should
instrument checkout failures and recovery, not treat every abandoned cart as lack of interest.

14. Differentiation thesis: the Eduvos
Opportunity Engine
The proposed Opportunity Engine begins with a practical user-experience rule: never end a legitimate
buying journey at “no stock” or “not available.” The platform should first protect the buyer’s intent:
find another seller, another slot, another campus, a similar option, or let the buyer publish a
structured request. Only after those fallbacks fail does the system treat the interaction as unmet
demand.
Unmet demand is then aggregated by campus, category, time and intent. A single failed search is
noise; repeated patterns become a signal. The system can surface that signal to existing sellers
(“students repeatedly ask for X”), to aspiring entrepreneurs (“this campus has demand and little
supply”), and to the Incubation Hub (“this category has demand but low fulfilment”). The marketplace
thereby becomes a market-sensing instrument.
The strongest version of the idea closes the loop by measuring what happens next. If a student
launches a new offer, the platform can see listing views, contacts, bookings, completion, repeat
customers and disputes. That evidence can improve business support. This is materially different
from an incubator asking only whether a student “made progress.”

• Do students understand and trust a wanted/request flow?
• Can opportunity signals be aggregated without exposing individual search behaviour?
• Would aspiring entrepreneurs act on demand evidence?
• Can the Incubation Hub turn the signals into concrete support?

student verification plus government-ID verification. Real identity stays private to the
platform/Eduvos; public identity can later become a brand storefront after the seller earns trust.
16. Marketplace modes in scope
The research scope is deliberately multi-modal because student economic activity does not fit one
transaction type. Products, services, food, rentals, second-hand goods, digital products, events and
gigs each have different data, fulfilment and dispute requirements. Forcing all of them into one
“listing” flow creates fragile design.
Shared listing core
Every listing can share seller, campus, title, description, media, category, price/pricing model,
availability, visibility, trust context, moderation status and timestamps. Subtypes then add
domain-specific requirements.
Subtype examples
• Product: stock, condition, pickup/delivery options, variants.
• Service: duration, availability slots, brief, booking, 30% deposit, milestones.
• Food: ingredients/allergens, preparation window, storage and compliance declarations.
• Rental: availability period, deposit, condition evidence, return deadline, late charges.
• Digital: file/version/licence, controlled delivery, download evidence.
• Event: capacity, tickets, waitlist, vendor applications, reminders.
• Gig/opportunity: brief, deadline, budget, application/quote process and approval rules.

17. Campus-first discovery and live
availability
The user’s strongest experience requirement is continuity: if one seller is unavailable, the platform
should keep working rather than tell the buyer to leave. This is a direct response to marketplace
liquidity problems: a marketplace fails when demand arrives but cannot find usable supply.
For products, “availability” should mean real stock plus pickup/delivery readiness. For services it
should mean bookable time. The interface can show states such as Available now, Available today,
Available after 15:00, Pickup today and Next available slot. These states should be backed by seller
configuration or inventory rather than decorative badges.
The campus-first ranking principle is: own campus first for physical convenience; wider Eduvos only
when needed. Digital services can be institution-wide by default. This reduces irrelevant results while
preserving the all-campus network effect.

18. Cross-campus fulfilment and designated
pickup points
When a product exists at another campus, the research concept allows three fulfilment paths: a
verified student courier transports it between campuses; the seller ships/transfers it to the buyer’s
campus; or the buyer arranges collection at the other campus. The marketplace should compare
these options by cost, expected time and eligibility.
For same-campus transactions, designated pickup points can reduce ambiguous meetups. The
research should compare official safe zones, staffed desks, smart lockers and seller/courier handoff
areas. Eduvos should approve physical locations and operating rules; the app should not infer or
publish sensitive campus safety details without institutional approval.
The student-courier concept is hybrid: verified students may apply to deliver, while Eduvos or the
Incubation Hub can create a higher-trust “approved courier” tier. The research must test safety,
insurance/liability, earnings, late delivery, lost items, food handling and what proof constitutes a
completed handoff.
Proof-of-handoff options to test
• Buyer one-time code / QR at designated pickup.
• Seller/courier and buyer timestamps.
• Photo evidence only when privacy-safe and necessary.
• Event logs tied to the order rather than exposing continuous location history.

19. Protected payments: the experience is
easy; the money movement is not
The user-selected experience is similar to Yaga: the buyer pays inside the marketplace; funds remain
protected; the seller fulfils; the buyer manually confirms receipt/completion; only then does the seller
receive the payout. Yaga demonstrates that this buyer-confirmation pattern exists in South Africa, but
its legal and payment-provider structure should not simply be copied. [R13]
The implementation should avoid Eduvos casually “holding client money” in its own bank account.
PASA states that organisations accepting payments from multiple parties for a client or disbursing
payments to multiple parties may need Third Party Payment Provider registration through a
sponsoring bank. The architecture should therefore use a regulated payment partner/subaccount/split
model and obtain legal/payment advice before launch. [R20] [R23]
Proposed transaction states
Requested → Accepted → Awaiting payment → Protected payment confirmed → Preparing / In
progress → Ready / Delivered → Buyer confirms → Completed / Payout. Alternative end states include
Cancelled, Refunded, Partially refunded and Disputed.
Services
The user-selected research policy is a fixed 30% upfront deposit for services, with the remaining 70%
protected until completion. If the buyer cancels after work starts, a moderator decides whether the
seller retains all, some or none of the deposit based on the agreed brief, timestamps, submitted work
and communications. This should be tested for fairness across very different service types.

20. The 2% commission problem: unit
economics must be explicit
The planned 2% app commission is attractive to students, but processor pricing creates a structural
question. At public standard rates, a R500 Paystack card payment is about R15.50 before VAT, while
a 2% platform commission is only R10. Payfast card pricing is higher. Some pay-by-bank rails can fit
below 2%, but not every payment method will. [R19] [R21] [R22]

21. Buyer protection, refunds, returns and
disputes
The platform’s trust proposition depends on clear failure handling. Mature marketplaces such as Yaga
and Bob Shop tie protection to transactions completed on-platform and define evidence, exclusions
and support processes. EduHackers should do the same. [R13] [R14]
Buyer-protection cases to support
• Non-delivery / seller never fulfils.
• Item or service materially differs from agreed listing/brief.
• Unsafe, prohibited or fraudulent transaction.
• Partial failure where only part of the order/service was delivered correctly.
• Returnable physical item with evidence and policy eligibility.

Human moderation
Disputes should be reviewed by a human moderator using order data, in-app messages, uploaded
evidence, delivery/handoff records, timestamps and the original listing/brief. AI can summarise
evidence, detect inconsistencies and prioritise risk, but the user has explicitly chosen human review
for dispute decisions.
Two-sided fairness
Buyer protection cannot become an easy refund-abuse mechanism. Buyer reputation should
therefore include payment reliability, cancellations, abusive dispute patterns and failed collections.
Seller reputation should separately reflect fulfilment, response time, complaints, repeat customers
and cancellations. Neither side’s score should be a single opaque AI number.
22. Trust architecture: verification,
reputation and progression
The proposed trust model has several layers rather than one five-star score. Identity trust comes from
Eduvos student verification plus government ID for sellers. Transaction trust comes from completed
orders, fulfilment, response time, cancellations, disputes and verified reviews. Business maturity
comes from sustained good standing and seller progression.
The user-selected storefront unlock is 10 successful sales + a recommended R1,000 cumulative
completed sales value + good rating/trust standing + no serious unresolved disputes or active
probation. R1,000 is a product-policy recommendation, not a regulatory standard. It is low enough to
avoid excluding low-ticket businesses while still requiring real marketplace activity.
SARS confirms that a sole proprietor can trade under a fictitious/trade name without creating a
separate legal entity. That supports allowing informal student brands without mandatory CIPC
registration, while formal registration can remain an optional future status. [R29]
The 2026 turnover-tax threshold for qualifying micro businesses increased to R2.3 million with a
R600,000 tax-free threshold, but the marketplace should not act as a tax adviser. It can provide
links/education when sellers approach formalisation milestones. [R30]

23. Trusted Seller benefits - earned
visibility, not paid boosting
The user rejected paid listing boosts. This is important because paid visibility could advantage
students who already have more capital. The proposed model gives stronger discovery privileges to
sellers who earn trust, while reserving exposure for new sellers so the marketplace does not become
permanently incumbent-dominated.
Benefits to research for Trusted Sellers
• Priority placement where relevance and availability are otherwise comparable.
• Eligibility for higher-value gigs / opportunities.
• Higher transaction or rental limits.
• Faster payout eligibility where the payment provider supports it and risk remains acceptable.
• Advanced analytics and business tools.
• Eligibility for selected subscriptions/recurring services.
• Priority eligibility for campus event/vendor opportunities.
Ranking fairness
Search should not simply sort by lifetime sales. A more defensible ranking model combines relevance,
campus, live availability, fulfilment quality, trust and controlled exploration for new sellers. The
weighting should be explainable and monitored for campus/category concentration.
24. Food marketplace research: convenience
creates higher safety obligations
Food can be a high-frequency student category, but it is not “just another listing.” The user wants
food allowed only where sellers list ingredients and understand food hazards. South African hygiene
regulations and labelling requirements create a strong case for category-specific onboarding,
ingredient/allergen disclosure, preparation/storage rules and institutional approval. [R27] [R28]
Proposed food controls to research with Eduvos/legal/health
• Mandatory ingredients and common-allergen fields before publishing.
• Preparation date/time and safe storage/serving guidance where relevant.
• Seller declaration that preparation location and practices meet applicable requirements.
• Higher-risk food categories routed for stronger review.
• Clear cancellation/refund rules for perishable orders.
• Delivery handoff times tracked for food orders without creating unnecessary location history.
AI role
AI can detect missing allergen fields, inconsistent ingredient claims or obviously disallowed listings,
but it cannot certify that a kitchen is hygienic. Compliance AI should therefore function as a
pre-publication checker and moderator assistant, not as a substitute for institutional and legal
requirements.

25. Prohibited items, AI moderation and
graduated enforcement
The platform needs a clear prohibited-items policy covering illegal goods, age-restricted or
institutionally inappropriate products, unsafe services, explicit/adult material, academic-misconduct
services and other categories Eduvos decides not to permit. The product should prevent publication
where rules are clear, rather than relying only on reports after harm occurs.
User-selected enforcement flow
AI/policy check → flag → notify seller → block/hide listing → moderator review →
warning/probation/temporary suspension → permanent removal for serious or repeated violations.
Buyers can face the same graduated enforcement for abuse, fraud, repeated no-shows or bad-faith
disputes.
Automated moderation makes mistakes. Low-confidence cases should be hidden pending review
rather than trigger a punitive account action. Every enforcement action should have a reason code,
evidence, timestamp, reviewer, appeal path and audit log. This is consistent with responsible-AI
principles that emphasise transparency, human oversight and risk management. [R31] [R32]
Policy design principle
The safest model separates content state from account punishment. AI can block a specific listing
quickly to reduce risk; higher-impact penalties should use human review or an appealable process.
26. Services: booking, 30% deposits and
academic-integrity boundaries
Services need different workflows from products. Live availability, booking slots, briefs, deposits and
completion evidence are central. The proposed default deposit is 30% for all services. The team
should test whether users understand what the deposit covers and whether the same rule feels fair
for tutoring, photography, design, coding assistance, repairs and event work.
Academic services should be permitted only where they support learning: tutoring, study coaching,
proofreading, concept explanation, coding guidance and design feedback. Services that complete
graded assessments, impersonate a student or enable cheating should be prohibited.
Higher-education academic-integrity guidance makes this distinction important, particularly where
AI-generated work is involved. [R39]
Service evidence trail
• Original listing and agreed brief/version.
• Scheduled appointment or milestones.
• In-app messages and agreed changes.
• Files/submissions delivered through or logged by the platform where appropriate.
• Buyer acceptance or dispute with evidence.
For large service jobs, the research can compare a single 30/70 split with multi-stage milestones, but
the user-selected baseline remains 30% upfront and 70% protected until completion.

27. Rentals and high-value items
Rentals extend marketplace value beyond ownership: textbooks, calculators, cameras, formal wear,
gaming equipment, tools and event gear can be temporarily shared. This introduces a different risk
model centred on deposits, return deadlines, condition evidence and damage/loss disputes.
Proposed rental flow
Rental request → availability confirmed → rental fee + refundable security deposit protected →
condition evidence captured → item collected → return due time tracked → automatic late charges if
overdue → return evidence → deposit released or dispute opened.
High-value rentals should have stronger requirements: additional identity/trust checks, higher
deposits, before/after photo or video evidence, stricter renter standing and possibly category-specific
transaction limits. Late fees should be disclosed before checkout and capped/reviewable to avoid
disproportionate penalties.
Research questions
• What deposit level feels fair by item value and category?
• What evidence is practical at pickup/return?
• How should normal wear differ from damage?
• When should the platform require a designated pickup point?
• How should courier damage be separated from renter liability?
28. Second-hand goods and digital products
Second-hand goods
Condition should be standardised rather than free-form: New, Like New, Good and Fair, with
mandatory defect disclosure and current photos. Electronics may require serial/model fields,
functionality checks and stronger evidence. The user does not want swaps/trade-ins; this keeps the
transaction model simpler.
Digital products
Digital products can include student-created templates, design assets, beats, presets, e-books,
coding tools and study resources, subject to copyright and academic-integrity rules. Files should be
versioned, scanned and licensed clearly. A buyer can receive access immediately after payment while
seller payout remains protected until confirmation.
Digital buyer protection creates a special abuse risk: a buyer could download a file and then claim it
did not work. The system should therefore retain download/access logs, file hashes/version history
and the original listing claims. Refund decisions should be evidence-based, not automatic.
Copyright and academic integrity
The marketplace should require sellers to confirm that they own or are authorised to distribute digital
content. Study resources that reproduce protected textbooks, leaked assessments or answer graded
work should be blocked. The platform can permit original notes or learning resources subject to
Eduvos academic-integrity policy.

29. Limited recurring services and
subscriptions
The user wants subscriptions only for a limited set of appropriate categories and only for Trusted
Sellers. This is a sensible restriction because recurring billing increases harm if the service
deteriorates or cancellation is unclear.
Candidate categories to research
• Recurring tutoring or study coaching.
• Selected ongoing creative/technical support where deliverables are clearly defined.
• Meal-plan or recurring physical supply only if food safety and fulfilment can be reliably governed.
• Other categories only after evidence and policy approval.
Controls
• Explicit billing period and renewal date.
• Simple cancellation and future-charge stop.
• Clear missed-session / missed-delivery rules.
• No long lock-in periods for students by default.
• Only Trusted Sellers in good standing.

30. Events, gigs and opportunity
marketplace
The user wants campus events integrated into the ecosystem. Students should be able to discover
events, RSVP, buy tickets, join waitlists and receive reminders. Sellers can apply for stalls or vendor
slots. Approved organisers can manage capacity, tickets and vendor applications. This creates a
direct connection between campus engagement and student commerce.
The gigs/opportunities layer allows approved societies, student organisations, campus stakeholders
and verified businesses to publish legitimate paid needs such as photography, graphic design,
catering, tutoring, web work or event support. Multiple verified sellers could respond with
proposals/quotes.
Critical institutional boundary
The platform must not be framed as a way to bypass Eduvos procurement. Stakeholder interviews
should explicitly ask which opportunities may be awarded to students through the marketplace and
which must follow formal procurement or contracting procedures.
Why events matter strategically
Events can seed marketplace liquidity. A campus market day, workshop or entrepreneurship event
can create concentrated buyer/seller activity, generate verified transactions, expose new sellers and
create measurable engagement for the Incubation Hub.

31. Social discovery: campus-first reels,
follows and recommendations
The user wants a TikTok-style short-video discovery feed, but the research goal should be commerce
discovery rather than recreating a generic social network. Sellers can post product/service reels;
organisers can post events/opportunities; buyers can post transaction-linked reviews,

across Eduvos.
Benefits to test
• Show products/services in a richer format than static listings.
• Give new sellers organic discovery opportunities.
• Promote events, gigs and campus activity.
• Create social proof through transaction-linked reviews.
Risks
• Popularity may overwhelm relevance and trust.
• High moderation volume for user-generated video.
• Attention-maximising ranking can conflict with student wellbeing and marketplace purpose.
• Commercial and non-commercial campus content can become confusing.
A safer research direction is a purposeful discovery feed: ranking uses campus, relevance,
availability, trust and freshness, with controls to prevent a few high-engagement sellers from
monopolising exposure. Paid boosts remain out of scope.
32. Group buying, rewards and campus
recognition
Group purchases can make sense where demand is naturally pooled: catering, printing, merchandise,
event supplies or transport-like services. A group order should define minimum participants, deadline,
price rule, cancellation conditions and what happens if the threshold is not reached.
The user is open to a points system but wants Eduvos to help define rewards. That is the correct
governance model. Points could recognise completed purchases, reliable selling, event participation,
referrals, courier reliability, verified reviews and Incubation workshop participation. They should not
become an unregulated cash substitute.
Two reward layers
• Eduvos-wide: consistent recognition and institution-level programmes.
• Campus-local: challenges, event participation, seller discovery and campus-specific partnerships.
Leaderboards
Campus recognition can include Trusted Sellers, highly rated services, reliable couriers, fast-growing
businesses and constructive community contributors. Rankings should be category-specific,
time-bounded and resistant to pure sales-volume domination. Some internal rankings may be better
kept private to avoid unhealthy competition or gaming.

33. Incubation Hub intelligence: from
dashboard to intervention
The user wants the Incubation Hub to actively use marketplace evidence. Marketplace performance
can automatically make a seller eligible for support; direct contact requires the seller to have opted
in. If not opted in, relevant support opportunities can still appear inside the seller dashboard.
Internal Business Health Score
The user wants the raw Business Health Score visible to Incubation Hub staff, not to sellers. It can
combine interpretable indicators such as sales trend, repeat customers, fulfilment, cancellations,
disputes, ratings, response time, demand signals and account activity. The score must show its
components and limitations rather than present a mysterious AI judgement.
Seller-facing guidance
Instead of exposing the internal score, AI can notify the seller with concrete observations: response
time is slipping, cancellations are rising, a frequently requested product is missing, or a particular
category is gaining demand. These prompts should be factual and explainable.
AI-assisted intervention
AI can recommend interventions to Hub staff - marketing support, pricing review, formalisation
guidance, funding readiness, stock/operations coaching or mentorship. Staff approve the
intervention. Then the AI can generate the user-selected 30-day improvement plan with daily/weekly
actions, measurable goals, checkpoints and progress tracking. The plan should remain editable by
staff and student.
34. AI as a visible compliance and
marketplace assistant
AI is not intended as a novelty chatbot. The proposed role is operational: make the marketplace safer,
easier to list on and more intelligent. NIST AI RMF provides a useful governance lens: map the
context, measure risk, manage controls and govern the system, with transparency and human
oversight. South Africa’s emerging AI policy direction likewise emphasises responsible, inclusive,
human-centred governance. [R31] [R32]
High-value AI use cases
• Listing completeness and policy checks before publication.
• Prohibited-category detection and moderation triage.
• Ingredient/allergen completeness checks for food.
• Duplicate/scam-pattern detection and suspicious transaction flags.
• Natural-language search mapped into filters.
• Demand clustering and opportunity summaries.
• Seller analytics summaries and factual coaching prompts.
• Incubation intervention recommendations and approved 30-day plans.
Human-control rules
AI may block or hold a specific listing where the policy match is clear, but account punishment,
disputes and high-impact decisions require a human-review path. AI outputs should carry reasons and
confidence; moderation decisions should be logged and appealable.

35. Privacy and POPIA: the platform should
know less than it can
The platform will handle identity, payments, messages, location context, transaction history, reviews
and potentially sensitive dispute evidence. POPIA therefore needs to influence data architecture, not
only the privacy policy. [R24]
Data-minimisation principles
• Public profiles never display raw student numbers or government ID details.
• Identity verification records are separated from public marketplace profiles.
• Analytics use pseudonymous user IDs wherever possible.
• Precise location is optional and task-specific; campus is the default location context.
• Courier live-location history should not be retained longer than operationally necessary.
• Dispute evidence has access controls and retention rules.
• Internal Business Health Scores are restricted to authorised Incubation staff.
Automated decision risk
Where AI or scoring materially affects access, suspension, opportunity eligibility or other significant
outcomes, EduHackers should avoid opaque automated decisions. Explainability, human review and
appeal are safer both ethically and under data-protection principles.
The architecture should maintain a clear record of purpose, lawful basis, retention, access roles and
deletion for each data class. Privacy should be part of the ERD and event taxonomy, not a final
checklist.

41. Payment architecture and compliance
research
Payment protection is both a UX feature and a regulated money-movement problem. Paystack
documents transaction splits and subaccounts specifically for marketplace-like scenarios. PASA
documents TPPP obligations for accepting or disbursing payments on behalf of multiple parties. [R20]
[R23]
Architecture principle
EduHackers should model an internal PaymentHold state without assuming Eduvos literally holds
money. The external PSP/payment partner remains the system of record for authorisation,
settlement, refunds and payout. The marketplace stores references and business state.
Payment research checklist
• Which PSP supports seller subaccounts / marketplace onboarding in South Africa?
• Can funds be protected until buyer confirmation under the provider’s terms?
• How are refunds and partial refunds represented?
• Who bears transaction fees?
• How quickly can seller payouts occur?

42. Consumer, electronic-transaction and
platform-policy implications
South African consumer and electronic-transaction law affect marketplace disclosures, transaction
terms, refunds and supplier information. The Hack Jam team should not attempt to become legal
counsel, but the product concept should expose the issues clearly and avoid promising policies that
contradict law. [R25] [R26]
Policy areas requiring institutional/legal sign-off
• Who is the contractual seller: student, Eduvos, or both in different contexts?
• How prominently must seller identity/contact information be disclosed?
• Cancellation/cooling-off rights for different transaction types.
• Return/refund exclusions for custom services, digital goods and perishables.
• Event ticket cancellation and organiser obligations.
• Rental deposits, late fees and damage deductions.
• Student courier liability and insurance.
• Food seller eligibility and campus health/safety requirements.
• Dispute appeal and record retention.
The safest product design makes terms contextual. A buyer renting a camera should see
rental/deposit/late rules; a buyer booking design work should see the service brief, 30% deposit and
cancellation rules; a food order should show ingredients, allergens and fulfilment timing.

A buyer should find a usable option quickly. Search success, fallback success, response time and

55. Conclusion
The market research changes the question from “How do we build a student marketplace?” to “What
does Eduvos uniquely gain from owning the marketplace loop?” The answer is not simply listings.
Existing platforms already show verification, secure payments, wanted boards, booking, analytics,
campus delivery and entrepreneurship support.
The stronger EduHackers concept treats commerce as evidence. Every search, fallback, request,
transaction, repeat purchase, cancellation and opportunity can help the marketplace become more
useful. Repeated unmet demand can reveal what students want. A student can test an offer against
that demand. A seller can earn trust and graduate into a stronger storefront. The Incubation Hub can
receive privacy-conscious evidence and approved AI recommendations, then support the student
through a human-governed intervention and 30-day plan.
That vision is ambitious. The right Hack Jam strategy is therefore to prove the loop, not build the
whole universe. The research should establish the real Eduvos problems first; the MVP should
demonstrate trust, discovery, protected transaction state, unmet-demand capture and incubation
intelligence; and the broader modes should be staged based on evidence.
